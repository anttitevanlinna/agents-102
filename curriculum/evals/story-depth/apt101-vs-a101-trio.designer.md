# Sibling read: product designer

*Persona: product designer, one product team, mid-size Nordic software company. Uses chat AI daily and well; has seen agents only in demos. Read both handbooks in full, in manifest order, maintainer and backing blocks stripped.*

## A, as I read it.

1. A is written about my actual week: the opportunity tree that dies on Monday, the interview quote, the prototype, the three of us arguing in planning. It starts by saying the post-it "was not naive", which is the first time a training has started by not insulting my craft.
2. It answers the question I don't say out loud. Building gets cheap and deciding doesn't, so the person who sat in the interviews gets *more* important. "A customer quote no customer said" is exactly the failure I'd catch and the PO wouldn't.
3. Three slides I'd remember on Monday: **"A customer quote no customer said"** (every quote in the tree carries interview, person and minute; I'm doing that next sprint), **"The designer's piece: a prototype that answers one question"** (Houde & Hill. The finished look got cheap, but choosing the question didn't), and **"The branch one of you found is often worth most"** (an agent merging our trees gives us the middle, smoothly).
4. Parts I'd tune out of: Cagan's four risks, Deming's tampering, the aligned-autonomy / future-leadership run near the end (that's the team lead's slide, not mine), and the double diamond and milkshake, which I could teach myself. The "wider, deeper and faster, what is your insight?" refrain comes back four times. By the fourth it's a jingle.
5. What worries me: I finish the handbook still not sure I could make an agent run overnight. "Six parts of an agent" is a list. I've read it, but I haven't felt it.

## B, as I read it.

1. B actually teaches how agents work, which is my real gap: memory folders, plan mode, subagents, a scheduled morning agent, a hallucination benchmark, an eval loop that runs while you walk away. I'd come out able to build something, not just talk about it.
2. But it is written for someone else. My "next big challenge" is meant to be a pricing redesign, an EU market entry, or "rolling out agentic ways of working to 300 engineers". The closing forum has a sponsor telling me "You are now agent builders." I'm not a CMO, and I'd be the one in the room quietly wondering who these examples are for.
3. Three slides I'd remember on Monday: **Mata v. Avianca and "Are you sure?" is another fluent answer** (the best-told cautionary case in either handbook), **"Mostly right, ten times over, is mostly wrong"** (85% per step, roughly 20% end to end; I'd draw that on a whiteboard), and **"Groundedness protects the floor, steering raises the ceiling"** (finally a name for the persona I keep rewriting).
4. Parts I'd tune out of: the security-skill packaging and runtime toggles (Code vs. Cowork, Save buttons, `/rename`), the four-window retriever choreography, the 20-agent forum, and the LinkedIn StoryBrand site (fun, but personal branding isn't why my team pays for three days). Lines like "a weapon" and "Damn, this is complex stuff" read as the oversell I discount by reflex.
5. It's long and individual. Almost everything is "your challenge, your memory, your agent". The team only arrives in Module 7. My team would have three people each building a private system.

## Which one I'd go to.

**A.** We're three people buying one training, and A is the only one that treats us as three people. The PO's piece, my piece and the team lead's piece sit side by side, and it ends with "on Monday the team decides, not the three of you." That's how decisions stick here. It keeps customers in the middle. "Without customer contact, agents build your guesses faster" is the one sentence I'd want my PO to hear from someone other than me. It also handles the things my legal team will ask about in the first meeting (customer names in interview notes, "the door you don't open") as product decisions rather than a security module. And it answers my private fear about my craft honestly, without hype.

What B does better: it puts my hands on the machine. A *tells* me agents drift, fabricate and need checks. B makes me build the benchmark, watch four detectors disagree, and keep a judge file that names its own blind spot. I'd come out of B knowing what an agent is. I'd come out of A knowing what it's for. B's evidence is also harder: two documented cases with court and audit records, where A's examples are mostly the author's own blog posts. Several of A's best slides ("Are you sure?", "Access is easy", "the door you don't open", the floor and the ceiling) also appear in B, and in B I've earned them with an exercise first.
