# Story craft: how a training gets frame, narrative, point of view, stance and depth

The method behind the storytelling quartet in `curriculum/evals/story-depth-rubric.md`. The rubric measures; this page says how to build. It applies to every training. Each training's choices live in its strategy doc, not here.

## The order

1. **Strategy first.** Write down the storyline, the hidden big points, who "us" is, and the stance on the future. Those are the source of everything below.
2. **Squint the titles.** Sequence the slide titles alone until they tell the story read cold. Bodies wait.
3. **Fix placement against the room.** Lectures sit beside exercises, so the beat order wins over title flow. A title judge flags placement but doesn't move it.
4. **Write the bodies** under titles that already work.
5. **Judges, then the benchmark.**

Building a deck bottom-up from frameworks produces a syllabus. A good syllabus scores about 50 on narrative however well it is sourced.

## Frame: one sentence every module is a case of

- State it early. Re-case it in each lecture. **Name where it breaks** near the end. A frame without a stated break caps around 80.
- Sources (Deming, Bainbridge, Torres) appear as what the lens shows, not as the subject.
- Examples: AE101 *agentic engineering is engineering*; Agents 101 the full agent picture; APT101 *building got cheap, deciding didn't*, which breaks at the Red Queen.

## Narrative: the student's journey, with a turn they live through

- **The student is the hero.** They arrive with their own problem and leave with something for their own team. The guide, the agents and the trainer serve that journey.
- **Recognition before naming.** The exercise produces the failure and the lecture names it afterwards. A lesson stated before the turn costs points; the same line after it earns them.
- **The ending is open and points forward.** The student finishes the story after the training. A judge rewarding a closed event ("the bet lost") is pulling toward the wrong shape. Tell judges the open ending is intended.
- Lecture-only handbooks lose narrative because the turn lives in the room. Count on that when you compare handbook scores with full-training scores.

## Point of view: a guide with scars, at the right moments

- **Real, dated, verbatim.** The maintainer's own words (essays, a first-person story such as AE101's `story-of-module-6`), quoted, never paraphrased into their voice, never invented. Backing `result:ATTESTED`. Name no employer or colleague.
- **The guide, never the hero:** empathy plus authority, "we've been where you are".
- **Three or four appearances:** the start, the turn, the close. More reads as a guest lecturer.
- **Narrated, not cited.** A block quote with a byline reads as one more source; the voice has to carry the slide.
- The top rung needs the narrator's own failure on the page. Only the maintainer can supply it.

## Stance: a chosen belief about the future, held as a question

- Stance is choosing what we believe about where this is going. Pose it as a question, not a prediction.
- The test is a position the training could lose a customer over, defended with a mechanism or a scar.
- **Thread it:** plant it early, sharpen it at the turn, answer it, ask it again at the close.
- Hedged lines ("safe enough, for now") and consensus maxims read as no stance. Bridge lines the strategy adopted on purpose keep their cost; don't sharpen them past what the strategy holds.
- Examples:
  - AE101: *a rule in context is not a rule in the output*.
  - Agents 101: *nobody knows where agents are going*.
  - APT101: *when models analyse wider, deeper and faster, what is your insight?*, answered as real strategy work.

## Depth: an idea whose meaning changes

- **Plant, complicate, pay off across modules.** A restatement scores zero.
- **One thread the student carries works best.** APT101's overnight digest is sent on Day 1, agrees with you on Day 2, and is caught by your own criteria on Day 3.
- Near-repeats dilute depth. Several titles saying "the agent knows only what you tell it" read as no development.

## Headers carry it first

- **The header carries the turn; the body carries the citation.** The style is distilled from AE101 in `curriculum/module-design/header-style-distilled.md`:
  - a law in four words with a physical verb;
  - demote the trusted artifact;
  - a semicolon that divides the labour;
  - relocate the control.
- The style goes on three to five voice-carrying titles per day. The rest stay plain.
- Bland shapes: count-teasers, noun lists, topic labels, imperatives that restate a borrowed framework while the twist hides in the body's last line.
- Every header still passes the squint and truth clauses in `check_lectures.md` §4.

## Measuring

- **Squint judges** read the titles only, cold. Tell them the intended shape: the student is the hero, the guide stands beside them, the ending is open and points forward, and placement follows the room. Score the quartet plus *forward*.
- **The handbook benchmark** is paired against AE101 in one context, on a 1–100 scale (`curriculum/evals/story-depth.md`). Read handbook scores beside full-training scores; they measure different things.
- **Before cutting a title, check the slide's reader scores.** A title judge sees nine words; the readers saw the slide.
- **An advisor pass between judge rounds is cheaper,** and it catches what title judges can't see: beat order, adopted lines, reader evidence.
