## Frame

**One sentence:** Output only gets trustworthy when you control what feeds the system and run an honest loop on it, because certainty is never on offer — and that control stops working the moment the loop has to run inside someone else's head.

Every module is a case of the same thing. M1: "Context is whatever you tell it... Same words. Different answer" (`lectures/context-is-king.md`) — generic output is starved input, proven on the student's own bio. M4 names the mechanism explicitly at training-scale: "You don't get *secure / not secure.* You get *safe enough, under these conditions, within these limits, for now*" (`lectures/practice-of-risk.md`) — the loop, not a fix, is the deliverable. M6 turns the same loop into infrastructure and states the integrity condition baldly: "A yardstick you rewrite is not a yardstick" (`lectures/evals-as-steering.md`).

Where the frame breaks, and the training says so: M7. Every prior module's loop worked because the student was the one curating, checking, rewriting. M7 hands the object to someone else, and the control point vanishes: "The interface is the first piece whose shape depends on somebody whose calendar you do not control... This one is not [yours to decide]" (`lectures/access-is-not-absorption.md`). The frame doesn't quietly fail here — the lecture names the exact place it stops applying.

## Narrative

Five sentences, with the turn: A student arrives wanting leverage on a real problem and gets it, fast — a memory that "sharpens instead of grows," three agents where one used to search, a judge that scores instead of a gut check. Every layer works, and every layer relocates the same unresolved question instead of answering it: is this actually right? By Module 6 the system is visibly succeeding — scores climbing, claims shrinking, a generator tightening itself round over round — and that is exactly when the training turns on its own protagonist: "Every clean week is real evidence the loop is working, and real erosion of your ability to tell when it stops working. Those are the same weeks" (`lectures/new-human-role-in-the-loop.md`). The obstacle was never a bad output; it was the good ones, arriving on schedule, wearing down the one skill that could have caught the next bad one. The training doesn't resolve this either — it closes on "Hold that doubt the way you held it then" (`lectures/where-is-this-all-going.md`), the same unresolved feeling from Module 3, still open at Module 8.

## Point of view

The narrator is an instructor-practitioner speaking in second person throughout, someone who has run this system on real work and watched where it bent, not a documentation voice. Three places the narrator shows itself: (1) `lectures/what-just-happened.md` — "Agreeable answers won the round the model was tuned on... The self-report is the same kind of output as the thing it is reporting on," a mechanism-level claim about the tool's own self-grading, stated as something learned by watching it happen, not asserted as doctrine; (2) `lectures/practice-of-risk.md` — "Damn, this is complex stuff. It will still be complex when the next agent gets built," a first-person register break mid-lecture; (3) `lectures/where-is-this-all-going.md` — "the people running this training do not know either," the narrator including itself inside the uncertainty it is teaching.

The narrator's own failure is on the page, twice, not once: M8's admission that the *easy* version of this training — one impressive demo agent — was considered and rejected as "the wrong thing to sell you" (`lectures/where-is-this-all-going.md` / M8 debrief), and M6's admission that the training's central mechanism (the loop) degrades the very judgment it depends on the longer it succeeds. This is a narrator naming what its own method costs, not just what the technology risks.

## Stance

Positions the training could lose a customer over:

1. **Plain text beats databases and tooling.** Defended with mechanism, not preference: "Language models are strongest at reading and writing text... Nothing sits between the model and what it's best at. Every fancier setup that promised to 'fix' this added a layer that the model had to work *around*" (`lectures/compounding.md`).
2. **Don't split agents; most of the time one prompt wins.** Defended with a falsifiable test against the reader's own excitement: "Ask yourself: *can I write one prompt that produces the same quality of output?* If yes, you didn't need to split... Resist it" (`lectures/when-to-split-an-agent.md`).
3. **Avoidance beats mitigation, even when it costs a sale.** Held against the training's own commercial interest, on the page: "it costs whoever sells you agents too, this training included: an agent that reaches less is a smaller thing to sell, and it is still the right call" (`lectures/practice-of-risk.md`).
4. **You cannot share an agent; "share the whole agent" is a vendor pitch.** Defended with a scar-shaped mechanism (accountability doesn't copy): "The fourth [part] does not copy at all, which is why the product being sold as a shared agent is always three of the four parts, with the fourth still sitting with whoever built it" (`lectures/access-is-not-absorption.md`).
5. **The impressive one-agent demo was the wrong thing to build for you.** Held against the training's own commercial interest a second time, more directly than #3: "We could have spent today building you one impressive agent to show on Friday... it would have been the wrong thing to sell you" (M8, `agents-building-agents.md`).

## The pair

Stance and self-doubt run together throughout, not as two separate registers. The plain-text position (#1) sits beside "It can only use what someone wrote down... most of what you know about your own company, nobody has" (`lectures/compounding.md`) — the training defends its own method and names its ceiling in the same breath. The security stance (#3) sits beside "'I can't tell' is a real answer" — the discipline it recommends is explicitly one that doesn't resolve to confidence. This reads as someone who has been there: the doubt is never used to soften a stance into a hedge (the positions stay sharp — "Resist it," "still the right call"), and the stance is never used to paper over the doubt (M6 and M8 both leave the unease standing, unfixed, at the close). Not a pitch, not a hedge — a practitioner's account of a method that works and costs something, told without picking only the flattering half.
