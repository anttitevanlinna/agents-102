## Frame

One lens: *your job stops being the doing and becomes steering the loop that does the doing — and the loop only gets better if you edit it, not if you're clever in the moment.* Three modules that only make sense through it: M2 "Compounding" — "It gets better by being edited, not by being clever"; M6 "Evals as Steering" — "The human moves one level up" (renamed "The human does not disappear"), "Steering raises the ceiling"; M1's closer "You felt context move" / "You act on the future to know what's real" — the first lived instance of the loop, before the training names it. No title names where the frame itself breaks — M4's "The best mitigation is the door you don't open" is restraint, not steering, and nothing in the skeleton flags that as the frame's edge. Titles show the lens recurring; they don't show it being tested against its own limit.

## Narrative

A builder leader wants to run agents at scale without either babysitting them or losing the plant. The obstacle: agents amplify whatever you bring, so competence has to move from doing the task to designing the system around it, and every shortcut (cleverness, one summarizer, an ungoverned score) quietly fails. The turn: by M6 the training's own evaluation apparatus becomes the thing under suspicion — "The gate is a claim too," "The judge names its own limit" — the fix from M5 is itself put on trial. Quoted title that is the turn: **"The gate is a claim too."** Titles alone show a shape (joy → unease → deepened unease → rescue → leverage → generosity → awe) that a student would feel as a turn, and the turn lands as the training's own machinery, not an outside villain — that's the 100-anchor case, but only one slide carries it, so it reads as asserted more than dramatized from titles alone.

## Point of view

A narrator who has run this loop before and is naming the traps as lived, not theoretical: "Why the retro was kind to you" (M1), "Could a competitor claim this?" (M2), "The doubt stays. Hold it." (M3 debrief). The narrator's own failure is on the page, not just the student's: **"The gate is a claim too"** and **"The judge names its own limit"** put the training's own instrument under the same doubt it's been asking the student to hold. That's a real self-indictment, not a hedge — titles show it directly.

## Stance

Positions this could lose a customer over, each carried by a title: (1) don't reach for multi-agent by default — **"Start with don't"**; (2) restraint beats capability as a security posture — **"The best mitigation is the door you don't open"**, which argues against selling "agents can do more," i.e. against the training's own upsell instinct; (3) a metric you can edit to pass is worthless — **"A yardstick you rewrite is not a yardstick"**; (4) the evaluation layer the training just taught you to trust is itself unproven — **"The gate is a claim too."** Titles show these as stated calls, and a couple carry their own mechanism in the phrasing itself (the yardstick line, the door-you-don't-open line argue by image, not just assertion) — but "defended" in the full sense (with a worked failure case) isn't visible from a title alone. Position #2 and #4 both cut against commercial interest: less selling of "do more," less confidence in the training's own grading.

## The pair

Someone who's been there: the training holds its own governing tools ("Steering raises the ceiling," "A yardstick you rewrite is not a yardstick") in the same breath as its own doubt about them ("The gate is a claim too," "The judge names its own limit") — stance and self-doubt on the same spine, not one without the other.
