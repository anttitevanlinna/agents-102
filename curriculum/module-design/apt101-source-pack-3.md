## 1. Rob Fitzpatrick: The Mom Test

**Book:** Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test: How to talk to customers & learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you* (2013; pre-order page captured April 2013). Paid book; **not opened**.
**Author's site (a):** https://www.momtestbook.com/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06. Sales page only: it does not state the three rules. Same for `/teachers` (opened 2026-10-06), which names the chapters to teach.
**Author's site, 2013 (b):** https://web.archive.org/web/20130405011145id_/http://momtestbook.com:80/ [practitioner direct]. Read 2026-10-06 in Wayback. Pre-order page with the original framing and table of contents; the sample chapters it linked were never archived.
**Author's blog (c):** Rob Fitzpatrick, "Customer convos are the carpenter's hammer // Pick the right tool for the job // Validation Quads v0.9", robfitz.com, 20 Oct 2022. https://robfitz.com/c/writing/customer-convos-are-the-carpenter-s-hammer-pick-the-right-tool-for-the-job-validation-quads-v0-9/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Transcription (d):** the book's own sentences, read 2026-10-06 on Goodreads quote pages https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/10670144-the-mom-test-1-talk-about-their-life-instead-of and https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/26235307. Reader-submitted transcriptions of the book, attributed there to Fitzpatrick; not checked against the book.

Quotes:

- (d) "The Mom Test: 1. Talk about their life instead of your idea 2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future 3. Talk less and listen more"
- (d) "The world's most deadly fluff is: "I would definitely buy that." It just sounds so concrete. As a founder, you desperately want to believe it's money in the bank. But folks are wildly optimistic about what they would do in the future."
- (d) "In short, remember that compliments are worthless and people's approval doesn't make your business better." And the recovery moves: "Getting back on track (avoiding bad data): Deflect compliments Anchor fluff Dig beneath opinions, ideas, requests, and emotions"
- (d) "Results of a good meeting: Facts — concrete, specific facts about what they do and why they do it (as opposed to the bad data of compliments, fluff, and opinions) Commitment — They are showing they're serious by giving up something they value such as meaningful amounts of time, reputation risk, or money Advancement — They are moving to the next step of your real-world funnel and getting closer to a sale"
- (b) "People say you shouldn't ask your mom whether your business is a good idea. That's true, but it misses the point. You shouldn't ask *anyone* whether your business is a good idea. At least not in those words."
- (c) "When customers have a strong understanding of a well-defined problem, conversations can double as both learning *and* validation. But if their "problem" is kind of murky, undefined, or in the future, then you're only going to get learning (not validation), and you'll need to use something else to validate, like a simple-lovable-complete v1 product."

**Summary.** The Mom Test is three rules for a customer conversation: talk about the customer's life, not your idea; ask about specific things that already happened, not general habits or opinions about the future; talk less. Its targets are three kinds of bad data: compliments, fluff (generic or future-tense claims such as "I would definitely buy that"), and opinions. A good meeting yields facts about what people do and why, and, once you are selling, a commitment or a step forward.

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating it as an interview script that forbids ever mentioning the product. The rules govern how you learn; Fitzpatrick's sales chapters (taught as "using commitment and advancement to validate early customer interest", per the teachers page) are about asking for time, reputation or money once the idea is on the table. Second misreading: assuming every question can be settled by talking. His 2022 post limits conversations to learning when the problem is "murky, undefined, or in the future".

**Drift.** (1) The third rule, "Talk less and listen more", usually drops out of the slide version. (2) "Compliments are noise" is close but softer than the source: in the transcribed wording compliments are "worthless" and the move is to "deflect" them, alongside anchoring fluff and digging beneath opinions. (3) Grading evidence on a ladder is our slide's frame. In what was read today, Fitzpatrick's own sort is bad data (compliments, fluff, opinions) against facts, commitment and advancement. Credit the ladder to us, not to him. (4) Fitzpatrick is writing a second edition (robfitz.com home page, 2026-10-06: "Currently writing Mom Test: 2nd edition"); recheck the rule wording when it ships.

## 2. Houde & Hill: what do prototypes prototype?

**Primary:** Stephanie Houde and Charles Hill (Apple Computer, Inc.), "What do Prototypes Prototype?", authors' version of the chapter in *Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction* (2nd ed.), M. Helander, T. Landauer and P. Prabhu (eds.), Elsevier, 1997. https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs247/2012/readings/WhatDoPrototypesPrototype.pdf [academic/research]. Opened 2026-10-06 (Stanford course-reading copy; the PDF's own footnote: "This article is published, in a different format, as Houde, S., and Hill, C., What Do Prototypes Prototype?, in Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction (2nd Ed.)").
**Failed:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/B9780444818621500820 (the publisher's chapter page) returns 403.

Quotes:

- "We propose a change in the language used to talk about prototypes, to focus more attention on fundamental questions about the interactive system being designed: What role will the artifact play in a user's life? How should it look and feel? How should it be implemented?"
- "We define prototype as any representation of a design idea, regardless of medium. This includes a preexisting object when used to answer a design question."
- "'Role' refers to questions about the function that an artifact serves in a user's life—the way in which it is useful to them. 'Look and feel' denotes questions about the concrete sensory experience of using an artifact—what the user looks at, feels and hears while using it. 'Implementation' refers to questions about the techniques and components through which an artifact performs its function—the "nuts and bolts" of how it actually works."
- "Current ways of talking about prototypes tend to focus on attributes of the prototype itself, such as which tool was used to create it … and on how finished-looking or -behaving a prototype is (as in "high-fidelity" and "low-fidelity" prototypes). Such characterizations can be misleading" … "It is important to recognize that the degree of visual and behavioral refinement of a prototype does not necessarily correspond to the solidity of the design, or to a particular stage in the process."
- "Integration prototypes are built to represent the complete user experience of an artifact." … "they are the most difficult and time consuming kinds of prototypes to build."
- Summary advice: "Know your audience. The necessary resolution and fidelity of a prototype may depend most on the nature of its audience." / "Know your prototype; prepare your audience. Be clear about what design questions are being explored with a given prototype—and what are not." / "Efficient prototypes produce answers to their designers' most important questions in the least amount of time."

**Summary.** A prototype is defined by the question it answers, not by the tool or the polish. Houde and Hill sort design questions into three kinds: role (what the thing does in someone's life and why it is useful), look and feel (what using it is like to the senses), and implementation (how it works inside). Any prototype sits somewhere in that triangle. An integration prototype covers all three and is the most expensive kind. The designer's job is to pick the prototype that answers the most important open question fastest, and to tell the audience what it does and does not test.

**Misreading to avoid.** Reading the three dimensions as stages (role first, then look and feel, then build). A role prototype can be a storyboard or a pizza box; a look-and-feel prototype can come first if the role is already clear. The authors' 3D space-planning example built role, look-and-feel and implementation prototypes at the same time, not in sequence: "It was more efficient to wait on the results of independent investigations in the key areas of role, look and feel and implementation than to try to build a monolithic prototype that integrated all features from the start."

**Drift.** (1) The model is usually taught as three types. The chapter has a fourth, integration prototypes, built to bring the three together. (2) "Fidelity matches the question" is a fair compression, but the paper is wary of the word itself: it calls "high-fidelity" and "low-fidelity" labels "misleading", defines fidelity as "closeness to the eventual design", and says the needed resolution and fidelity "may depend most on the nature of its audience". On a slide, "match the prototype to the question, and tell the audience which question" is closer than "match fidelity to the question". (3) The slide gloss "a rough prototype tests value, polish tests nothing yet" is ours. The paper's claim is narrower: refinement "does not necessarily correspond to the solidity of the design", and a finished-looking prototype may be made early, for example for market research. It also warns that "inexperienced audiences tend to believe them to be more functional than they are". (4) "Role" is not the same word as "value" (Cagan, pack 2 §4): role is the function the artifact serves in a user's life. Keep the authors' word on the slide.

**Backing line:**
- houde-hill-1997 `[checked:2026-10-06 result:OK due:none]` https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs247/2012/readings/WhatDoPrototypesPrototype.pdf — [academic/research] role / look and feel / implementation, plus integration prototypes; prototype = any representation of a design idea; hi-fi/lo-fi labels misleading; fidelity depends on audience; say what a prototype does and does not explore. Authors' version of the 1997 Handbook chapter (Elsevier page 403s).

Opened 2026-10-06.

Quotes:

- Book p. xi: "These hypotheses cover three types of risk. First, that customers aren't interested in your idea (desirability). Second, that you can't build and deliver your idea (feasibility). Third, that you can't earn enough money from your idea (viability). You test your most important hypotheses with appropriate experiments."
- Book cover leaf: "You're holding a field guide for rapid experimentation. Use the 44 experiments inside to find your path to scale."
- (a) "Map the most important assumptions with the least amount of evidence to focus your experimentation." … "Behind every new business idea hides risky assumptions. If proven false, these important and unproven assumptions can make or break your business."
- (a) "David first learned the 2x2 from working with Jeff Gothelf, Josh Seiden and Giff Constable. The Lean UX book illustrates how to use the 2x2, but teams still struggled with what type of experiment to run. - (b) "Desirable - Do they want this?" / "Viable - Should we do this?" / "Feasible - Can we do this?" … "Draw the X axis first and map 'have evidence' vs 'no evidence'. Then draw the Y axis and map 'important' vs 'unimportant'." … "Experiment on the top right quadrant to generate evidence for these important and unproven assumptions." … "Defer commitment on everything below the X axis for now."
- (b) "The shared understanding generated by the mapping conversation is much more valuable than the map itself."
- (c) "Basically the Test Card forces you to make the following things explicit: What needs to be true for your idea(s) to work (aka hypothesis, assumption, or simply guess)? How are you going to test if that hypothesis is true or false? What are you going to measure to (in)validate your hypothesis? How does success look like? What's the threshold?"
- (c) "At Strategyzer, we talk to customers a lot and continuously conduct experiments before we launch something new. Yet, in the early days, we didn't learn as much as we should have despite talking to tons of customers. We were all over the place. That's why we invented the Test Card for ourselves."

Named experiments, as the book's index lists them (index PDF, read 2026-10-06):

- under **Discovery experiments**: "Feature Stub, 156–159" with "404 test, 160–161"; also "Customer Interviews", "Explainer Videos", "Pretend to Own", "Product Box", "Speed Boat", "paper prototypes", "online ads", "link tracking", "email campaigns", among others.
- under **Validation experiments**: "Concierge, 248–253", "Wizard of Oz, 284–287", "simple landing page, 260–265", "mock sale, 288–293", "presale, 274–277", "Single Feature MVP, 240–243", "Split Test, 270–273", "crowdfunding", "letter of intent", "pop-up store", "Extreme Programming Spike", among others.
- Elsewhere: "Assumptions Mapping, 36–39"; "Test Cards, 45–46"; "Learning Cards, 54–55, 62"; "Evidence strength, 52–53, 57".
- **Not in the index:** "fake door" and "smoke test" (searched both; zero hits; the nearest entries are "Feature Stub", "404 test" and "simple landing page").

**Summary.** Break an idea into hypotheses of three kinds: desirability (do they want this?), viability (should we do this?) and feasibility (can we do this?). Map them as a team on two axes, important against unimportant and evidence against no evidence. Test the important ones with the least evidence first. For each test, write a Test Card first: the hypothesis, the test, the measure, and the threshold that counts as success. The book then offers a library of experiments, split into discovery (is the general direction right?) and validation (confirm it with strong evidence).

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating the map as the output. Bland's worksheet says the conversation is worth more than the map, and that the map is "a living document" to be redrawn as evidence comes in. Second misreading: running an experiment that does not match the assumption's kind. The worksheet warns "run experiments that match the desirable, viable and feasible assumptions, otherwise you won't generate valid evidence."

**Drift.** (1) **Which experiments the book names.** Concierge and Wizard of Oz are named validation experiments. "Fake door" and "smoke test" have no entry in the index. By name, the nearest index entries are Feature Stub with its 404 test (discovery) and Simple Landing Page (validation); their pages were not read, so check the book before equating either with "fake door". "Smoke test" in this pack comes from Ries (pack 2 §6, "a simple AdWords smoke test"). On a slide citing the book, write "feature stub" or "landing page"; if the slide says "fake door", do not attribute that name to Bland. (2) **Count.** The book's own cover leaf says 44 experiments; Strategyzer's book page (2026-10-06) says "43 beautifully illustrated, detailed experiment types". Say "over forty" or quote the book. (3) **Test Card origin.** It predates the book: Osterwalder introduced it on the Strategyzer blog in March 2015. Credit the Test Card to Strategyzer and Osterwalder (2015), and assumptions mapping to Bland. (4) **Assumptions-mapping origin.** The 2x2 itself came from Lean UX (Gothelf, Seiden) and Giff Constable; Bland's additions were the desirable / viable / feasible colours and the axis labels. His worksheet credits the themes to "Larry Keeley & IDEO". (5) **"Riskiest assumption".** Bland's own words are "important and unproven" and "leap of faith assumptions"; "riskiest assumption" is the slide's paraphrase of the top-right quadrant. It is a faithful paraphrase, but quote his axis words. (6) **Viability.** The worksheet's question is "Should we do this?", which includes "this aligns to our vision because…". That is broader than "can we make money".

## 4. Steve Krug: do-it-yourself usability testing

**Primary (a):** https://sensible.com/rocket-surgery-made-easy/ [practitioner direct], Krug's page for *Rocket Surgery Made Easy: The Do-It-Yourself Guide to Finding and Fixing Usability Problems* (New Riders, 2010). Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** the book's table of contents and sample chapter ("Call me Ishmael"), https://sensible.com/downloads/rsme-toc.pdf and https://sensible.com/downloads/rsme-samplechapter.pdf [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (c):** the book's handouts, from https://sensible.com/download-files/ [practitioner direct], all opened 2026-10-06: test script https://sensible.com/downloads/test-script-web.pdf; observer instructions https://sensible.com/downloads/instructions-for-observers.pdf; "Things a therapist would say" https://sensible.com/downloads/things-a-therapist-would-say.pdf; checklists https://sensible.com/downloads/checklists.pdf.
**Supporting:** https://sensible.com/dont-make-me-think/ and its table of contents https://sensible.com/downloads/DMMT-Revisited-TableOfContents.pdf [practitioner direct] (*Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, 3rd ed., 2014; chapter 9 "Usability testing on 10 cents a day"). Opened 2026-10-06. The DMMT sample chapter is chapter 4, not the testing chapter.
**Not opened:** the body of either book beyond the sample chapter.

Quotes:

- (a) "after years as a usability consultant I'm convinced that they're the single best way to improve the user experience of a product." … "They think usability testing requires a lot of time, effort, and expertise. But they're wrong."
- (b) Table of contents: "chapter 3 A morning a month, that's all we ask: A plan you can actually follow" / "chapter 8 Mind reading made easy: Conducting the test session" / "chapter 9 Make it a spectator sport: Getting everyone to watch and telling them what to look for" / "chapter 10 Debriefing 101: Comparing notes and deciding what to fix".
- (b) "this book is only about one particular kind: simple, informal, small-sample, do-it-yourself usability testing (sometimes known as discount usability testing)." … "I didn't invent any of this. Usability testing has been around for a long time, and a lot of people—Jakob Nielsen being the most vocal and influential—have been advocating "discount usability testing" for at least twenty years."
- (b) "The kind of testing this book describes is not for making things foolproof to use; it's just for making them easier to use."
- (c, script) "The first thing I want to make clear right away is that we're testing the site, not you. You can't do anything wrong here." … "As you use the site, I'm going to ask you as much as possible to try to think out loud: to say what you're looking at, what you're trying to do, and what you're thinking."
- (c, observers) "Each of the three sessions will last about 50 minutes, with a ten-minute break in between." … "During the break between sessions, use the attached sheet to jot down the three most serious usability problems you noticed in that session." … "where we'll compare notes and decide what usability problems we're going to fix in the next month."
- (c, therapist) "While the participant is doing the tasks, to maintain your neutrality you're going to be saying the same few things over and over, which turn out to be the same kind of non-directive things a therapist typically says to a patient." Examples: "What are you thinking?" / "Is that what you expected to happen?" / "What would you do if I wasn't here?"

**Summary.** Krug's method is small and regular: one morning a month, three participants, each doing realistic tasks while thinking out loud, with the team watching from another room. Each observer writes down the three worst problems after each session. Over lunch the team compares notes and picks what to fix before next month. The facilitator stays neutral and does not help: the point is to see what people do without someone beside them.

**Misreading to avoid.** Turning think-aloud into an opinion interview ("do you like this page?"). Krug's script asks people to narrate what they are looking at, trying to do and thinking, and his facilitator lines are non-directive prompts, not questions about preference. Second misreading: testing without observers or a debrief. In the handouts, watching is the team's job, and the morning ends with a decision about what to fix.

**Drift.** (1) "Test with five users" is Nielsen's line, not Krug's. Krug's handouts plan three sessions in a morning, repeated monthly. "Three users" is stated here from the handouts (three sessions, Participants #1–#3); the book's own argument for three was not read. (2) The monthly cadence is chapter 3's title, "A morning a month, that's all we ask"; the popular telling often keeps "small sample" and drops the regular schedule. (3) Krug credits discount usability testing to Nielsen and says he invented none of it. A slide that pairs them should give Nielsen the method and Krug the do-it-yourself plan. (4) Scope: Krug says this testing makes things "easier to use", not "foolproof"; it is not for safety-critical systems.

## 5. Desirée Sy: parallel design and development tracks

**Primary:** Desirée Sy (Autodesk, Inc., formerly Alias), "Adapting Usability Investigations for Agile User-centered Design", *Journal of Usability Studies*, Vol. 2, Issue 3, May 2007, pp. 112–132 [practitioner direct: first-person case study in a peer-reviewed journal]. **Failed:** https://uxpajournal.org/adapting-usability-investigations-for-agile-user-centered-design/ (404; the journal site now runs a different system at `/index.php/jux`) and https://www.upassoc.org/upa_publications/jus/2007may/agile-ucd.pdf (404).

**Summary.** At Alias, Sy's user experience team ran design work in its own track beside development, staggered ahead of it. Designs were prototyped and usability-tested at least one cycle (two to four weeks) before developers built them, and contextual inquiry for later workflows ran at least two cycles ahead. Validated designs were handed to the developer track, and built versions were tested again to catch drift from the design. Large designs were cut into cycle-sized "design chunks". The two tracks were joined by daily contact, not only by the hand-off.

**Misreading to avoid.** Reading the two tracks as two teams that meet only at a hand-off. Sy says designers "need to communicate every day with developers" and take part in the daily scrum. The other misreading runs the opposite way: assuming the design track decides whether to build at all. In this paper the release's goals are set in Cycle Zero, and the design track works out how to build what is already planned, then checks it.

**Drift.** (1) **Name.** Sy's terms are "parallel tracks", an "Interaction Designer Track" and a "Developer Track"; "dual tracks" appears once. "Dual-track agile", "discovery track" and "delivery track" do not appear in the paper (full-text search; the only "discover" hit is "discoverability"). Those labels came later and from other authors, who were not opened today. (2) **What runs beside delivery.** The slide gloss "discovery runs beside it" stretches the source. Sy's parallel track is interaction design plus usability investigation (testing prototypes, contextual inquiry) for features already on the release plan. Deciding what is worth building at all sits mostly in Cycle Zero. Quote it as "design and usability testing one cycle ahead, contextual inquiry two cycles ahead". (3) **Credit.** The paper cites the parallel tracks to Miller (2005) and Sy (2005) [13, 14]. Credit "Sy and Miller, Alias, 2005–2007" rather than Sy alone. (4) **Hand-off shape.** "Developers receive validated designs" is a staggered hand-off, one cycle ahead. A slide that shows both tracks working on the same item in the same sprint misdraws it.
