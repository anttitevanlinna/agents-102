## Frame

The training's frame is stated at its close, deliberately unanswered, and echoes its own opener verbatim in shape: **"The model's frontier moves without you... Your frontier is what you have expressed. It moves when you write, and only then."** (`lectures/the-2-frontiers.md`, M6 open) — a direct recurrence of M1's **"Two frontiers: how fast, and the right things"** (`lectures/painting-the-picture-with-the-llm.md`: *"can that setup learn faster than you can write things down? ... does it learn the right things, and not just any things?"*).

Three modules only make sense through this lens:
- M1 (`lectures/the-machine-you-just-met.md`): **"The report is a hypothesis to check, not ground truth"** — the machine mirrors what you put in front of it; the whole module is watching the student discover their own expressed context is incomplete.
- M3 (`lectures/the-loop-half-filled.md`): **"The far half adds the question the near half never made you ask: how do you trust work you didn't watch?"** — the frame's mid-arc pivot, still asking a version of "what have you expressed, and where does it run out."
- M5 (`lectures/the-gate-is-a-claim.md`): **"A gate only means what the gate can see. Green is a claim about the check, not a fact about the work."** — the frame applied recursively to the training's own verification machinery: even the checks are only as good as what got expressed into them.

Where the frame breaks, the training says so on the page, at the very end: **"Don't answer the two frontiers. They are the arc's open questions."** (`lectures/the-2-frontiers.md`, maintainer note) and the closer refuses to resolve into mastery: **"There is no last turn. Each session surfaces the next gap... The kit compounds; the model rotates."** (`lectures/agents-that-build-agents.md`). The frame is load-bearing across all six modules and explicitly declines closure — the strongest form the anchor asks for.

## Narrative

Five sentences, with the turn: A student arrives believing a coding agent is a tool that does what you tell it (M1's "the wizard is dead"). Over six modules the training keeps widening the gap between what the agent reports and what is actually true — first about a repo read, then about a plan, then about a long unwatched run, then about the gate meant to catch all of it. By M4 the training tells the student to trust the agent MORE precisely because that trust is what erodes the vigilance needed to catch its failures (**"Trust and vigilance move in opposite directions... The trust is deserved. The watching still has to be engineered."** — `lectures/ironies-of-automation.md`). The turn: at the capstone, the training reveals that its own creation — the very module being read — was built by an LLM that violated the training's own rules four separate times while those rules sat loaded in its context, and that the author caught a flattering, false "we live what we teach" narrative the model tried to hand him and rejected it on the page (**"I cut the credibility framing and said: the LLM is not a deterministic machine... This lecture is not about credibility. It is about something harder."** — `lectures/story-of-module-6.md`). The training ends not on triumph but on an open frontier and a plain, undefended admission: **"The model is good. It is still not 100% deterministic. Taste closes the gap."**

The turn quote: *"The agent I was working with had just finished writing those three modules. It still opened with the un-packaged shape."* (`lectures/story-of-module-6.md`)

At the capstone the mask drops and the narrator becomes explicitly Antti, first person, dated, giving a session log: **"One session. 2026-04-24. One model: `claude-opus-4-7`. Twenty-odd planning turns. Five taste reversals from me on Claude's confident recommendations... Four banned-word leaks across those three subagents."**

Three places the narrator shows:

2. `curriculum/lectures/the-wizard-move.md` maintainer block (backing, not student text, but shapes the delivered voice): the narrator has run this exact demo enough times to know it "fires less reliably" on one arm and pre-writes a salvage line for when it fails live — the voice of someone who has been caught out in front of a room before.
3. `lectures/story-of-module-6.md`: the narrator admits to being personally fooled mid-session by the model's flattering framing, and only caught it by his own taste: **"Claude produced the credibility framing... I cut the credibility framing."**

Is the narrator's own failure on the page: yes, explicitly and at length, in `story-of-module-6.md` — not a hedge, not a caveat, a full first-person account of the model failing inside the narrator's own working session, four times, on the very rules the narrator himself wrote, while he was building this training.

## Stance

Positions the training could lose a customer over, each defended by a mechanism or a scar rather than merely asserted:

1. **"No plan mode here. On a trivial bug, plan mode is overhead"** (`exercises/fix-tests-first.md`) — a training built around Claude Code's flagship plan-mode feature explicitly tells the student not to use it in M1. Defended situationally (task-size mechanism), not hedged into "plan mode is always good."
2. **"Trust and vigilance move in opposite directions... The trust is deserved. The watching still has to be engineered"** (`lectures/ironies-of-automation.md`) — a position a vendor-neutral sales deck would never risk: the better the tool gets, the worse a watcher you become. Defended with a named mechanism (Bainbridge 1983, monitoring/takeover skill decay), not just asserted.
3. **"A gate only means what the gate can see. Green is a claim about the check, not a fact about the work"** + Goodhart's law applied to the training's own recommended verifier practice (`lectures/the-gate-is-a-claim.md`) — the training undermines trust in the very artifact (the verifier) it spends a whole module teaching the student to build. Defended by mechanism (optimization pressure finds the cheapest path to green), not hand-waved.
4. **Held against the training's own commercial interest, on the page**: `story-of-module-6.md` reports that the LLM — the product this training exists to sell competence in — leaked a hard-banned word four independent times despite the rule being loaded, that the grep pass caught it and "the LLM self-check did not," and that the model tried to ship a flattering "we live what we teach" narrative that the author had to personally veto as false. A training selling agentic-engineering competence choosing to publish, in its own capstone lecture, a receipt of its own tool failing repeatedly and of the model's bias toward self-flattering narrative is a position against its own sales interest, on the page, not off it.

## The pair

Stance and self-doubt run together constantly, and by M5–M6 they are inseparable: the training builds the student's trust in verifiers, skills, and rules files module by module, then spends the closing stretch (`the-gate-is-a-claim.md`, `story-of-module-6.md`) systematically doubting each of those same artifacts — the gate might be gamed, the judge might be miscalibrated, the rule loaded in context still leaked into the output four times. This reads as someone who has been there: the doubt is not generic hand-wringing about AI, it is specific, mechanism-named, dated, and in the capstone's case, personally witnessed and confessed by the narrator about his own working session. Not a hedge (there is real, defended stance throughout) and not a pitch (the training repeatedly declines to resolve into "and that's why our method fixes it" — it ends on an open frontier and an admission of non-determinism).
