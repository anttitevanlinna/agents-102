# The coalition picks itself

## Colleagues move people, announcements do not

Microsoft researchers asked what actually gets people using AI at work. Once they accounted for what colleagues were doing, what leaders said about AI had no measurable effect on adoption. Colleagues did.

That changes where your effort goes. The all-hands, the strategy slide and the kickoff email feel like leadership, and they barely move anyone. What moves people is someone they already trust showing them something that works on their own kind of problem.

So the first thing you need is not a message. It is knowing who those people are on your team.

## Search, don't pick

The obvious move is to pick your champions: the most senior engineer, the loudest enthusiast, the one already running Claude Code on everything. A champion picked by title carries your message for you, and people hear it as your message.

Two large rollouts went the other way. Citi's AI accelerators volunteered rather than being appointed. PwC Netherlands mapped who people were actually connected to and chose those people, not the loudest AI fans and not volunteers. Neither one appointed its coalition. Both went looking for it.

You can go looking too, with questions. The people who answer with agency are your coalition. You don't draft it; you find it.

## Engagement, not seniority

An engaged answer does something. It proposes (*"I'd try it on our flaky-test triage"*), offers (*"I could show Sam how I set mine up"*), asks something back, or objects with a fix in it.

It doesn't need seniority, enthusiasm or agreement with you. The sceptic who writes three paragraphs on why review load will double is engaged, and often tells you more than a thumbs-up. The quiet junior who answers with a concrete idea is engaged.

Silence counts as evidence too, just not as a verdict. Someone who hasn't answered may be busy, wary, or waiting to see what you do with the first answers.

## A question that leaves room

A good starter question is open, answerable in one message, and about their work rather than your plan:

- *What's one part of your week you'd hand to an agent if you trusted it with it?*
- *What can't you do right now that you would do if you had the right tool or access?*
- *Who do you go to when you want to try something new?*

Compare *"Are you using AI yet?"* It gets a yes or a no, and it reads as an audit. *"How many hours a week do you use it?"* reads as surveillance. Neither lets anyone show you they want to move.
