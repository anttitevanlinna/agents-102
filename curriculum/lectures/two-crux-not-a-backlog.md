# Two crux, not a backlog

A shortlist of good moves feels like progress. Every item on it is plausible, and that is the trouble: a list of plausible moves is a backlog, and a backlog gets worked in the order whoever asks loudest sets.

## The crux is the hard part you can move

Richard Rumelt borrowed the word from rock climbing, where the crux is the hardest move on the route. In strategy, it is the part of your challenge that is both surmountable and promises the greatest progress. Leaders become strategists, he argues, when they work on challenges rather than goals.

## A crux names a mechanism

"Get the team using agents" is a goal. "Culture" is a category. "The two seniors who review every change have no time to try agents, so nobody sees it done on our own code" is a crux. It names what blocks, so you can see what moving it would release.

## Two, not one or three

Rumelt writes about one crux. This training asks you for two, as its own choice. With one, when it stalls, everything stalls. With three, you are back to a backlog. Two gives you a second place to push when the first pushes back, and both still fit in sentences you can say to your team.

## Each crux carries what it beat

A crux picked without alternatives is a guess you cannot revisit. Each of yours goes into the Decision Journal with the candidates it beat and what you give up. When the evidence turns, you reopen the call instead of starting over.

## A crux starts as a hypothesis

One week of answers is enough to pick a crux, not to prove it. Your memory holds each one as a hypothesis, and what your people say over the coming weeks promotes it or sends it back.
