## Frame

**One-sentence frame:** Agents 101 is a training about context — what you feed the system, what you withhold from it, and who stays responsible for the gap between the two — and every module is a variation on that single move.

Three modules that only make sense through it:

1. **getting-going.md / context-is-king.md (M1)** — the frame is stated outright and demonstrated mechanically: "The same question. Two answers. What makes the difference?" (context-is-king.md). The whole module's payoff line is context-shaped: "Phase 1 is fine. Phase 6 is yours" (personal-site-with-guardrails.md) — the only variable between a generic site and a genuinely-theirs one is the context fed in.
2. **building-agent-systems.md / compounding.md (M2)** — context gets a second axis, shelf life: "Context isn't only about countries... What if the context was a file the agent reads at the start of every conversation?" (context-is-king.md, M1 seed) pays off directly in "In the full agent picture, Module 2 adds shelf life. Context stops being something trapped in one conversation. It becomes memory" (compounding.md).
3. **security.md / practice-of-risk.md (M4)** — the frame does not just recur, it inverts: where M1–M3 taught *add more context*, M4 teaches *withhold it*: "Should the agent read from that mailbox? If not, don't connect it" and "The cheapest, most reliable way to reduce risk on an agent is to not open the door in the first place." Same variable (what the agent gets to see), opposite instruction.

**Where the frame breaks:** the break is real but not narrated. The training never says, in so many words, "Module 1 told you to give the agent more; Module 4 tells you to give it less — here's the tension." A student who is paying close attention notices the reversal themselves; the text lets the reversal do the work silently rather than naming it as a seam in its own idea. That is the gap between an 80 and a 100 here — every module reads as a case of context, but the training does not stop to say where its own governing idea stops applying.

## Narrative

A builder leader walks in chatting with an AI like everyone else and gets the same generic paragraphs everyone gets; a demo of two chat windows shows them why ("Same words. Different answer," context-is-king.md) and by the end of the first hour they own a site that finally sounds like them ("Phase 1 is fine. Phase 6 is yours"). They turn that trick on a real problem, build a memory that gets sharper instead of longer, then split the work across agents that are supposed to disagree with each other — and walk out of that module carrying a briefing they can't fully vouch for, told flatly to "hold the doubt" and not fix it yet. Then the ground shifts hardest: they are made to feel, in the room, that "certainty is a fantasy you inherited," that nothing they've built can be proven safe the way old software could be proven correct, and that the honest answer is a loop, not a guarantee. Two modules later a judge and a scoreboard give the doubt somewhere to go, an eval loop keeps sharpening the work while they are out of the room, and by the close of Module 8 the training delivers its actual turn: **"The destination is not 'the human disappears.' That is the lazy story. The better story is stranger: the human moves one level up"** (new-human-role-in-the-loop.md).

The turn is the training's own reframe of its subtitle, not a failure it confesses to — the student is promoted, not broken. That caps this below the top anchor: it is a turn the student lives through, in the room, at stake (their own real work challenge), but it is not the training's own failure landing on the page.

## Point of view

Who is speaking: a practitioner who has run this loop before, on real systems, and knows exactly where it goes wrong — not a marketing voice narrating capability, and not a neutral textbook voice. Three places the narrator shows itself:

1. "You built something. It works. Would you bet your job on it being safe? Probably not. And that feeling, that specific, uncomfortable, *I'd like to know and I don't* feeling, is the most plain thing in the room today" (practice-of-risk.md) — first person to the room, naming a feeling the narrator assumes because they've had it.
2. "You cannot hire three agents and expect the output to be three times as good... please, for the sake of your sanity, write the one prompt" (when-to-split-an-agent.md) — the exasperated-mentor register of someone who has watched people over-build multi-agent systems and paid for it in coordination cost.
3. "Nobody knows where agents are going. Not Anthropic, not your board, not the people running this training" (what-just-happened.md) — the narrator explicitly includes itself among those without an answer, refusing the authority position a vendor training defaults to.

Is the narrator's own failure on the page? Not a named, specific incident ("I built X and it broke"). What is on the page is the narrator's own *uncertainty* — about the field's direction, about whether its own multi-agent enthusiasm should be trusted, about whether its own security check is sufficient ("Concluding that the agent check replaces security ships a breach"). That is a narrator disclosing what it doesn't know, which is one notch short of disclosing what it got wrong.

## Stance

Positions the training could lose a customer over, each defended by mechanism or scar rather than asserted and left:

1. **Multi-agent, the thing this exact module is teaching, is usually the wrong call.** "Inside a workflow, start with don't... If a single well-shaped agent with a single well-shaped prompt can produce what you need, do that. Most of the time, it can" (when-to-split-an-agent.md). Defended by mechanism: three concrete tests (access, dialect, stance) plus "the test that catches the bluff" — can one prompt do it?
2. **Security theater ends where the security check starts, not where it stops.** "Mitigations are layered, not replacements... Concluding that the agent check replaces security ships a breach" (security.md). Defended by naming the specific failure mode it prevents (a false sense of closure).
3. **The simple, unsexy setup beats the fancy tooling that could be sold instead.** "The simplest possible setup beats the fancy ones, because it respects what the model actually does well... Every fancier setup that promised to 'fix' this added a layer that the model had to work *around*" (compounding.md). Defended by mechanism (plain text is what LLMs are strongest at reading/writing).
4. **"Share the whole agent" — the shape a vendor would pitch — is refused outright.** "'Share the whole agent' is a vendor pitch and is not on the list" (personal-to-team.md). Asserted more than defended with a mechanism in that line alone, but the surrounding absorption-bottleneck argument ("access is not absorption") carries the weight.

Held against the training's own commercial interest: #1, #2, and #3 all point the same direction — a paid training for a consultancy that could profit from selling more agents, more elaborate security packaging, and more tooling instead argues, on the page, for fewer agents, layered (not replaced) human security judgment, and the plainest possible setup. #3's line is explicit about the tension: "the one that sounds too much like common sense to charge for" (practice-of-risk.md, on the best mitigation being the one you don't need).

## The pair

Stance and self-doubt run together throughout, most visibly in M3–M4: the training holds a real position (multi-agent earns its keep only under three named conditions; security is a discipline, not a proof) and, in the same breath, tells the student to sit with what it cannot resolve ("Hold the doubt. Name it to yourself. Don't fix it here"; "Certainty is a fantasy you inherited"). This reads as **someone who has been there** — both stance and doubt are present, and neither cancels the other; the doubt is what makes the stance credible rather than salesy, and the stance is what keeps the doubt from curdling into a hedge. It is not a pitch (stance only, no acknowledged limits) and not a hedge (the training does commit to positions it could lose a room over — "write the one prompt," "don't connect it," "not on the list" — rather than staying vendor-neutral).
