(three named mechanisms by which a plausible plan passes as good) and "Push reach past what you can check and you have not delegated more. You are checking less." (reach/calibration, unnamed)
  - M3, `curriculum/exercises/author-test-strategy-skill.md` — "same-window self-charity" (names why self-grading in the same context is weak)
  - M3, `curriculum/lectures/the-loop-half-filled.md` — "The agent loop is a closed-loop controller... Cut the feedback signal... and it drifts" and "Control is interrogation... You can always read more; you can never read all."
  - M4, `curriculum/lectures/the-far-half.md` — "when the agent takes a hundred steps alone, what makes you trust the result at the end?" (explicit unanswered question — removes the tight feedback loop entirely)
  - M4, `curriculum/lectures/what-keeps-a-long-running-session-going.md` — "It declares done on the part it managed... the piece it quietly dropped goes unmentioned." (new failure shape: confident partial success)
  - M4, `curriculum/lectures/ironies-of-automation.md` — "Trust and vigilance move in opposite directions... The trust is deserved. The watching still has to be engineered." (the sharpest complication: earning trust correctly erodes the human's own ability to catch the next miss)

A gate nobody has verified is a gate trusted on vibes" and "Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" and "One session is a sample... react on recurrence, not on noise" (this reflexively turns the checks the student just built into new hypotheses needing their own verification)
  - M6, `curriculum/lectures/story-of-module-6.md` — Antti's own account: a hard-banned word slipped past four separate LLM instances despite the rule sitting in every context window; a false quote attribution passed three LLM verification passes and was only caught by source-verification; a self-contradicting closing line survived four LLM authors and was only caught by a human-persona simulation. "A rule in context is not a rule in the output."
- **Paid off:** M5, `curriculum/lectures/the-gate-is-a-claim.md` — "The delegation frontier" named explicitly (2×2 of reach × calibration), with the formal law: "The frontier moves outward only as fast as the gates behind it." This retroactively names the mechanism the student has been living since M2's unnamed "reach vs calibration" language and M3's Big Idea ("Trust isn't watching harder. It's verification you can run without being there.").
- **Governor:** No single governor — a stacked set, each firing at a different scale: M1 "ask for a ranked list, spot-check a claim"; M2 "push back twice, walk unresolved branches"; M3 "ask the tool to name its own weakest part, then push past the first answer"; M5 "match the verifier shape to the failure, and independently re-verify the verifier (hold-out check, compare to your own judgement, repeat across sessions)"; M5 "react on recurrence, not on noise." The training never collapses these into one sentence — arguably a minor cost to "applicable in practice" (see Factors).
- **Counter-voice:** M4 `ironies-of-automation.md` is the strongest: the very act of correctly earning trust in the agent degrades your ability to catch its next failure — a direct doubt cast on the training's own central move (build checks, earn trust, extend reach). M5 `the-gate-is-a-claim.md` extends this to the checks themselves: "A gate nobody has verified is a gate trusted on vibes" — even the trained student's own hard-built verifiers are suspect until independently checked. M6 `story-of-module-6.md` is the most concrete instance: the training's own authors, using the training's own rules, produced the exact failures the rules were meant to prevent, multiple times, undetected by the LLM doing the work.
- **Forward-looking claim:** M4's `the-far-half.md` — "what makes you trust the result at the end?" — an explicit open question, not answered until M5, and even then answered with "it's a claim, verify the verifier" rather than a formula. M5's bitter-lesson bullet in `the-gate-is-a-claim.md` — "Today's right procedure... is superseded too. Retire what the next model outgrows, add what it needs" — framed as a standing instruction to keep revising, not a forecast (no dates, no calendar; maintainer notes confirm this was a deliberate anti-prediction framing). This is a genuine QUESTION-shaped forward stance: the training explicitly declines to say when or how the procedure will need to change, only that it will.

### Learning 2 — Compounding must prune as much as it adds, or the system you're building collapses under its own bloat
One-line claim: "Every session should leave something durable for the next one — but a rules file, a skill kit, or a memory store that only ever grows becomes the next session's problem; compounding means adding AND cutting."

- **Planted:** M1, `curriculum/trainings/agentic-engineering-101/getting-going.md` Big Idea — "Your first session should leave something behind that the next one can use. Otherwise you have a clever assistant and no compounding." Built concretely via `./CLAUDE.local.md` across all four M1 exercises.
- **Complicated:**
  - M2, `curriculum/lectures/how-instructions-grow.md` — "Rules have a ceiling... The cure is not better rules; it is where the rules live... A file that is only ever added to never shrinks; when a later session disproves a rule, take it out." First explicit doubt cast on pure accumulation. Also: "Prohibitions stop; taste steers" — not just how much, but what kind of rule compounds well.
  - M3, `curriculum/lectures/skills-from-the-frontier.md` — "A skill outlives the thread it came from" and "Don't make general what you don't practice yourself" — compounding extends from personal (CLAUDE.local.md) to team-durable (skill), with a discipline on what earns that promotion.
  - M3, `curriculum/lectures/the-loop-half-filled.md` — "The compound ladder: fix → memory → skill → system" — names the ladder retroactively, after the student has already climbed two rungs (memory in M1, skill in M3) — a strong earned-not-announced structure.
  - M4, `curriculum/exercises/walk-and-send-off.md` — "Filling the worst two or three gaps is worth more than closing them all" — the same prune/triage discipline applied prospectively (before a gap becomes a rule, not just after).
  - M5, `curriculum/lectures/hooks-always-fire.md` — "Must happen goes in a hook; recommended stays in a prompt" — a structural distinction on what kind of durable artefact suits what kind of rule (not just prune, but sort by mechanism).
- **Paid off:** M6, `curriculum/exercises/spot-gaps-build-the-loop.md` — "Rules-files have a half-life. Adding rules is only half of it; subtracting the dead ones is the other half." The exercise forces an actual, evidenced rule deletion from a real two-run diagnosis — the first time "pruning" becomes a concrete required action rather than a stated principle. `curriculum/lectures/composing-the-workflow.md` reframes the whole thing as workflow composition: "Your job moves from reading every intermediate output to choosing the skills, their order, and the instructions each one carries" — compounding's endpoint is curatorial judgment, not accumulation.
- **Governor:** "Fill the worst two or three, not all five" (M4); "cut the one rule the diagnosis killed, no more" (M6); "must-happen goes in a hook, taste-shaped stays in a prompt" (M5) — a genuinely actionable triage rule at each stage.
- **Counter-voice:** Mild, but present — `how-instructions-grow.md`'s own admission: "This training stops short of the full system: rules that prune themselves, evidence that routes itself to the right file... How those parts run is for your team to automate." The training names the limit of its own solution rather than pretending the ladder is complete.
- **What it says about the future:** Mostly a governor/practice claim rather than forward-looking about the world. The closest thing to a forward claim is `agents-that-build-agents.md`'s "There is no last turn. Each session surfaces the next gap... The kit compounds; the model rotates" — a structural/open-ended claim, not a specific prediction.

### Learning 3 — The near-half's tight feedback loop (watch every step) cannot scale to long-running, unattended work — trust has to transfer from presence to designed structure
One-line claim: "As soon as work runs longer than you can watch, 'I checked it myself' stops being available, and what replaces it (packaging, gates, evals) has to be built in advance, not applied after the fact."

- **Planted:** M4, `curriculum/lectures/the-far-half.md` — "Long-running is not more of the same... When the agent runs for an hour without you watching, that quick feedback goes quiet." This is really the same underlying mechanism as Learning 1, viewed as a structural/temporal shift rather than an epistemic one — flagging it separately because the training itself treats it as a distinct beat (map's near/far halves, a named structural discontinuity) even though at the level of "big learning the student carries out" it substantially merges with Learning 1 by M5. Recommend the synthesis treat Learning 1 and Learning 3 as one learning with two faces (epistemic: claims decay; structural: presence doesn't scale) rather than two separate learnings — noting this explicitly here rather than double-counting in Factors.

## Audience fit

The learnings belong to a software-engineer IC, not a leader/CEO/CTO reading. Every governor is a concrete Tuesday-morning move on the IC's own machine and repo: push back on a diff, write a failing test, invoke a skill as a subagent, build a verifier shaped to a named failure, cut one dead rule. The vocabulary throughout is mechanism-level (closed-loop controller, hooks vs prompts, Goodhart's law on a gate, worktrees, plan.md) rather than org-level (no "align your team," no "drive adoption," no roadmap language as the substance of a learning). The one place org/team language appears — "cross personal → team," `where-the-rule-could-live.md`'s team-kit shapes, the wall metaphor in `learn-from-the-test.md` — is consistently framed as something the IC *chooses* to do later, after proving a rule/skill on themselves first ("A rule promoted before it works on you is a rule your team has to live with"), which keeps the locus of action on the individual engineer rather than reframing the student as someone who manages other people's work. Pass.

## Weakest factor

Forward-looking is the factor with the thinnest evidence relative to the others. The training is very good at holding open questions momentarily (M4's "what makes you trust the result at the end?", M6's "don't answer the two frontiers") but it resolves most of its own suspense within a module or two rather than leaving the student holding a live, unresolved question about where the *model* itself is headed. The bitter-lesson bullet in `the-gate-is-a-claim.md` is the one place the training explicitly says its own answers will expire, but it is a single bullet at the tail of a dense closing lecture, competing with five other laws on the same slide (Goodhart, tampering, judge-calibration, sample-size, delegation-frontier), and per that lecture's own maintainer notes it is fighting for space against a hard word-cap. The smallest concrete strengthening: let the M4 `the-far-half.md` question ("what makes you trust the result at the end?") stay explicitly open a beat longer into M5 — right now `reading-the-return.md` and `the-gate-is-a-claim.md` answer it almost immediately with a full mechanism (three failure modes, delegation frontier, bitter lesson) in the same module; one more line near the M5 close naming what would falsify today's answer (beyond "the next model" as the trigger) would give the forward-looking claim a genuine falsification condition rather than a general expiry notice.
