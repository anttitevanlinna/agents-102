# Writing style

How AE101 prose is written: who is writing, who is reading, and the sentence rules that keep one file readable on all three surfaces it feeds. The rule compendium (`check_writing.md`) carries the exceptions and the judge conventions. This is the writer's page.

## Who is writing

Five voices. Not all five in every sentence. Lead with the one the sentence's job needs.

- **Boris Cherny.** Factual and direct. Platform truth, technically precise. Leads any sentence that states what the tool does.
- **Roger L. Martin.** Logical and strategic. Names the frame, then the alternative. Leads a reframe with a choice inside it.
- **Seth Godin.** Generous and social. Peer warmth without flattery. Leads openers and anything that gives the reader status.
- **Rory Sutherland.** Counterintuitive reframe. Leads the one sentence per lecture that turns the obvious reading over.
- **Risto Siilasmaa.** Optimistic about the future and acting on it. Leads send-offs and forward beats. Never leads a beat that deliberately keeps a doubt open.

Wrong registers: the L&D coach, the cold spec sheet, the motivational poster.

## Who is reading

One lecture file feeds three readers.

1. The room, with the trainer talking over the slide.
2. A student reading the deck alone, weeks later.
3. A stranger reading the theory handbook, who never enrolled.

Write for the third reader. Prose a stranger can follow is always speakable, and the trainer compresses it live. Prose written as the trainer's cue card never expands itself for anyone else.

The test is the muted read. Read the slide with the trainer's audio off. Every sentence has to work.

## Sentence rules

- **One subject per slide.** The header names it and the body keeps it. If the header says "The LLM mirrors your stance," the first sentence is about the LLM and you, not about the history of autocomplete.
- **Every sentence has a verb.** A noun stack ("The same question, two answers.") tells the speaker what scene to perform. Give the reader the scene: "Ask two fresh sessions the same dinner question and they answer differently." Fragments are allowed only as an enumeration after a verbed sentence has named the set.
- **Every pronoun has its noun on the slide.** "Context is whatever you tell it" fails when no *it* is on the slide. Name the noun: the session, Claude, the agent. Pick one name for the machine per slide and keep it.
- **One sentence mode per slide.** Declaratives, questions, or instructions. Switch at the slide edge, never inside it. A question slide is a beat; a question inside a declarative slide is a wobble.
- **"You" is the reader.** Never "a practitioner" or "the sharp engineers" standing in for the person reading. Third person is for other people.
- **Stage directions go to the maintainer block.** "Take a guess before it runs," "this one runs on the screen," "Let's go" are the trainer's, not the page's.
- **One aphorism per lecture.** Say the thing once, at its most concrete, then at most one sentence that makes it portable. Stacked one-liners stop being heard.
- **Short sentences. No em dashes.** Break the thought in two.
- **Bullets for parallel items, paragraphs for a line of thought.** Six phases, five things a plan has, three pressures: bullets. A slide that argues one point in sequence reads as paragraphs, same words. Bulleted narrative is a deck printout; the wizard move is the model.

## Where this binds

Lectures, exercises, modules, and every page the theory handbook or the slides layout builds from them. Rules with exceptions and carve-outs live in `check_writing.md` (§4 register, §28 muted read) and `check_slides.md` (§1 referents, §4 demo beats).
