## 1. Teresa Torres: continuous discovery, interview snapshots, assumption testing

**Primary (a):** https://www.producttalk.org/continuous-discovery-mindsets/ [practitioner direct]. Teresa Torres, "The Best Continuous Discovery Teams Cultivate These Mindsets", Product Talk, 10 Oct 2018 (talk script, Industry Product Conference). Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** https://www.producttalk.org/interview-snapshot/ [practitioner direct]. "The Interview Snapshot: How to Synthesize and Share What You Learned from a Single Customer Interview", 21 Feb 2024. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (c):** https://www.producttalk.org/assumption-testing/ [practitioner direct]. "Assumption Testing: Everything You Need to Know to Get Started", 18 Oct 2023. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Supporting:** https://www.producttalk.org/product-discovery/ ("Product Discovery Basics", 18 Aug 2021; `/continuous-discovery/` redirects here) and the Product Talk glossary entry https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-continuous-discovery/ [practitioner direct]. Both opened 2026-10-06.
**Not opened:** the book *Continuous Discovery Habits* (2021).

Quotes:

- (a) "I define continuous discovery as weekly touch points with customers, by the team building the product, where they conduct small research activities, in pursuit of a desired product outcome."
- (a) "We don't want to save up all of our questions for a monthly interview. That overwhelms our customers and it holds up our product decisions."
- (a) "I often see teams who are great at research, but don't meet their outcomes. Some don't even ship a product. That's because their research isn't in service of a desired outcome."
- (b) "An interview snapshot is a one-page template that helps you quickly capture what you learned from a single interview." … "Ideally, the team would create an interview snapshot immediately after the interview while the stories are still fresh." Its elements: name, photo, quick facts, a memorable quote, opportunities, insights, and "An experience map that reflects the story that you collected in the interview".
- (c) "The key to considering multiple options while still moving quickly is to stop testing whole ideas, and to start testing the underlying assumptions that your ideas depend upon." … "Assumptions fall into five categories: desirability, feasibility, usability, viability, and ethical."
- (c) "As a team, work to define success upfront, before you run your assumption test. Think through what types of results you would need to see for your solution to succeed and then draw a line in the sand."

**Summary.** Continuous discovery is a cadence, a cast and a purpose together: at least weekly contact with customers, done by the people building the product, in small activities, aimed at a product outcome. Each interview is synthesised the same day into a one-page snapshot built around the customer's story. Ideas are not tested whole; the team lists the assumptions underneath them across five categories, tests the riskiest, and sets the success line before running the test.

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating "weekly" as the whole definition, with research handed to a research team or a vendor. Torres's definition names "the team building the product" and "in pursuit of a desired product outcome"; research without the outcome is exactly the failure she describes in quote (a3). Second misreading: arguing over which category an assumption belongs to. Torres: "it's not fruitful to spend time debating whether or not a specific assumption is a desirability assumption or a usability assumption. The point is to generate assumptions across the categories".

**Drift.** (1) The popular "talk to customers every week" drops two of the four clauses (who, and toward what). (2) Product Talk's own glossary paraphrases the book as "in pursuit of a desired outcome"; Torres's 2018 wording says "desired product outcome". Quote the 2018 page, not the glossary. (3) Cross-pack: Torres lists five assumption categories including *ethical*; Cagan (§4 below) keeps four risks and folds ethics into business viability. Do not merge the two lists on one slide as if they were one author's.

## 2. Josh Seiden: outcomes over output

**Primary:** Josh Seiden, "AI Makes the Output. You Own the Outcome.", *What's The Outcome* (his Substack), 6 May 2026. https://joshuaseiden.substack.com/p/ai-makes-the-output-you-own-the-outcome [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Supporting:** "Case Study: Outcomes for Climate Change", same Substack, 24 Sep 2025. https://joshuaseiden.substack.com/p/case-study-outcomes-for-climate-change [practitioner direct]. Read 2026-10-06 in the Substack RSS feed (full post text).
**Supporting:** https://www.joshuaseiden.com/books [practitioner direct], the book page for *Outcomes Over Output* (2019). Opened 2026-10-06.
**Failed:** https://joshseiden.com/ (the URL in the brief). HTTPS fails on a self-signed certificate; the HTTP version is a meta-refresh to joshuaseiden.com.
**Not opened:** the book itself. Its widely quoted wording, "a change in human behavior that drives business results", was **not** found on any Seiden-owned page opened today.

Quotes:

- (Substack 2026) "An outcome is a measurable change in human behavior that creates value."
- (Substack 2026) "you can build a product that meets every requirement, passes every test, passes QA, but still creates no value for users and customers." … "You only create value when: someone uses your product (or service) to do something new or someone uses your product (or service) to do something they already do in a better way."
- (Substack 2025) "outcomes are valuable changes in human behaviors, ones that make a positive contribution in terms of the impact we're trying to create." And the question to get there: "when we've achieved this impact, what will people be doing differently?"
- (Books page) "Mostly, we simply ask teams to build features—but features are the wrong way to go. We often build features that create no value. Instead, we need to give teams an outcome to achieve."

**Summary.** Seiden separates three things: output (what the team makes), outcome (a change in what people do), and impact (the larger result the organisation wants). Teams are given an outcome to reach rather than features to build, because a feature can ship to spec and change nothing. The working question is what people will be doing differently once the impact is achieved.

**Misreading to avoid.** Using "outcome" for any metric that sounds better than "features shipped", such as revenue or NPS. In Seiden's frame revenue sits at *impact*; an outcome is a behaviour, by named people, that can be observed and counted. A second misreading treats outcomes as goals for the business only: in his wording the change has to create value, and his test is whether users do something new or do something better.

**Drift.** The phrase most slides carry is "a change in human behavior that drives business results". Seiden's own current wording (2026) is "a measurable change in human behavior that creates value", and in 2025 he ties outcomes to *impact*, not "business results". Quote the Substack wording with its date, or open the book before using the "business results" version.

## 3. John Cutler: the feature factory

**Primary:** John Cutler, "12 Signs You're Working in a Feature Factory", originally on Medium/Hacker Noon, dated **Nov 16, 2016** there. The Medium and hackernoon.com URLs return **403** to curl; the original text was read 2026-10-06 in the Wayback snapshot https://web.archive.org/web/20180108012534id_/https://hackernoon.com/12-signs-youre-working-in-a-feature-factory-44a5b938d6a2 [practitioner direct].
**Republication:** https://cutle.fish/blog/12-signs-youre-working-in-a-feature-factory/ [practitioner direct], Cutler's own blog, "Published: November 17, 2016". Opened 2026-10-06. The twelve signs are word-for-word identical to the 2016 snapshot (diffed).
**Follow-up:** John Cutler, "12 signs you're working in a feature factory — 3 years later", Amplitude blog (his employer at the time; byline Cutler). https://amplitude.com/blog/12-signs-youre-working-in-a-feature-factory-3-years-later [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.

The original twelve, by their bold lead-ins (each is followed by a short gloss in the post): **No measurement. Rapid shuffling of teams and projects (aka Team Tetris). Success theater around "shipping" with little discussion about impact. Infrequent (acknowledged) failures and scrapped work. No connection to core metrics. No PM retrospectives. Obsessing about prioritization. No tweaking. Culture of hand-offs. Large batches. Chasing upfront revenue. Shiny objects.**

Quotes:

- Origin: "I started using the term when a software developer friend complained that he was "just sitting in the factory, cranking out features, and sending them down the line.""
- "No measurement. Teams do not measure the impact of their work. Or, if measurement happens, it is done in isolation by the product management team and selectively shared. You have no idea if your work worked"
- "No PM retrospectives. Product managers do not conduct regular retrospectives on the quality of their product decisions and compare expected benefits to actual benefits. Developers have "passing tests", but product managers do not."
- "Obsessing about prioritization. Mismatch between prioritization rigor (deciding what gets worked on) and validation rigor (deciding if it was, in fact, the right thing to work on)."
- 3-years-later: "One of the nuances of the original feature factory post was that placing prescriptive "build this" type bets may be the right approach."

**Summary.** A feature factory is an organisation whose unit of value is the shipped feature. Its symptoms are mostly about what happens *after* shipping: nobody measures, nobody checks expected against actual benefit, work is never removed, and the team moves on to the next item. Three years on, Cutler stressed that teams improve step by step and that heavy work-in-progress drives many of the symptoms.

**Misreading to avoid.** Reading it as "shipping a lot is bad" or as a charge against engineers. The symptoms are about missing validation, with product management named directly ("Developers have "passing tests", but product managers do not"). Cutler's follow-up also warns against all-or-nothing fixes: "Lots of teams take an all-or-nothing approach to overcoming their feature factory tendencies. Based on my work with teams, this is rarely how you make progress."

**Drift.** (1) The popular telling makes "feature factory" a pure insult. Cutler's own follow-up says prescriptive "build this" bets "may be the right approach" in some contexts, for example a fast follow in a well-known domain. (2) Date: the original platform shows 16 Nov 2016, his blog shows 17 Nov 2016. Say "November 2016".

## 4. Marty Cagan: the four big risks

**Primary (a):** Marty Cagan, "The Four Big Risks", SVPG, 4 Dec 2017. https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** Marty Cagan, "Product Risk Taxonomy", SVPG, 10 Jul 2023. https://www.svpg.com/product-risk-taxonomies/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Not opened:** *INSPIRED*, 2nd ed. (2018).

Quotes:

- (a) "value risk (whether customers will buy it or users will choose to use it)" / "usability risk (whether users can figure out how to use it)" / "feasibility risk (whether our engineers can build what we need with the time, skills and technology we have)" / "business viability risk (whether this solution also works for the various aspects of our business)"
- (a) "The Product Manager is responsible for the value and viability risks, and overall accountable for the product's outcomes. The Product Designer is responsible for the usability risk, and overall accountable for the product's experience – every interaction our users and customers have with our product. The Product Lead Engineer is responsible for the feasibility risk, and overall accountable for the product's delivery."
- (a) "Tackle the big risks early – especially value risk and business risk" … "Figure out solutions collaboratively – engineering, design, and product, working side-by-side"
- (b) "For example, business viability includes ethical risk, go-to-market risk, and compliance risk, to name a few that are especially important." … "any of these risk taxonomies can work, and any of them are far better than using none at all."

**Summary.** Before building, a team asks four questions: will people choose it, can they use it, can we build it, and does it work for our business. Each risk has a named owner: the product manager owns value and viability, the designer owns usability, the lead engineer owns feasibility. The work on all four is done together and early, especially value and viability.

**Misreading to avoid.** Turning ownership into a hand-off ("PM signs off value, then design, then engineering"). The same page says solutions are figured out "collaboratively… working side-by-side". Ownership says who answers for a risk; it does not mean the others stay out of it.

**Drift.** (1) The first edition of *INSPIRED* (2008) had three (valuable, usable, feasible). Cagan split viability out in 2018 because teams "would focus on customer value, but would gloss over business value." A "four risks since forever" slide misdates it. (2) Cagan does not claim the taxonomy matters more than using one; in 2023 he calls IDEO's desirability/viability/feasibility a good fit "for consumer products" and says any taxonomy beats none. (3) Ethics: see §1 Drift (3).

**Backing lines:**
- cagan-four-risks-2017 `[checked:2026-10-06 result:OK due:none]` https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/ — [practitioner direct] value, usability, feasibility, business viability; PM owns value + viability, designer usability, lead engineer feasibility; tackle big risks early, collaboratively.
- cagan-risk-taxonomy-2023 `[checked:2026-10-06 result:OK due:none]` https://www.svpg.com/product-risk-taxonomies/ — [practitioner direct] three-to-four-risk history (INSPIRED 2008 → 2018); ethics, go-to-market, compliance sit inside viability; IDEO taxonomy compared; any taxonomy beats none.

## 5. The Scrum Guide (November 2020)

**Primary:** Ken Schwaber & Jeff Sutherland, *The 2020 Scrum Guide*, HTML port at https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html [practitioner direct], which states it "is a direct port of the November 2020 version available as a PDF". Opened 2026-10-06. Quotes below rejoin the HTML port's hard line wraps; wording is unchanged.

Quotes:

- "The entire Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint. Scrum defines three specific accountabilities within the Scrum Team: the Developers, the Product Owner, and the Scrum Master."
- "The Scrum Team consists of one Scrum Master, one Product Owner, and Developers. Within a Scrum Team, there are no sub-teams or hierarchies. It is a cohesive unit of professionals focused on one objective at a time, the Product Goal." … "self-managing, meaning they internally decide who does what, when, and how."
- Product Goal: "The Product Goal describes a future state of the product which can serve as a target for the Scrum Team to plan against." … "The Product Goal is the long-term objective for the Scrum Team. They must fulfill (or abandon) one objective before taking on the next."
- Sprint Goal: "The Sprint Goal is the single objective for the Sprint. Although the Sprint Goal is a commitment by the Developers, it provides flexibility in terms of the exact work needed to achieve it." Set at Sprint Planning: "The whole Scrum Team then collaborates to define a Sprint Goal that communicates why the Sprint is valuable to stakeholders."
- Product Owner: "The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team." … "The Product Owner is one person, not a committee."
- Scrum Master: "The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide." … "The Scrum Master is accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness."

**Summary.** The 2020 guide speaks of three *accountabilities*, not roles. The Developers own the Sprint plan, quality and daily adaptation toward the Sprint Goal; the Product Owner owns value and the Product Backlog, including the Product Goal; the Scrum Master owns the team's effectiveness. Each artifact carries a commitment: the Product Goal for the Product Backlog, the Sprint Goal for the Sprint Backlog, the Definition of Done for the Increment.

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating the Sprint Goal as the Product Owner's target handed to the team. The guide calls it "a commitment by the Developers", defined by "the whole Scrum Team", and fixes the goal rather than the scope: when work turns out different, Developers "negotiate the scope of the Sprint Backlog within the Sprint without affecting the Sprint Goal."

**Drift.** (1) "Roles" is pre-2020 vocabulary: the 2020 guide does not use the word "role" for these at all (full-text search: zero hits); it says "accountabilities". (2) "Self-organizing" also has zero hits; 2020 says "self-managing". (3) Velocity, story points and user stories are often taught as Scrum; none of the three appears anywhere in the guide (full-text search: zero hits). (4) The Product Goal is a 2020 addition and is widely skipped in practice; it is a commitment in the guide, not an option.

HTML port of the Nov 2020 PDF.

## 6. Eric Ries: build-measure-learn and the MVP

**Primary (a):** Eric Ries, "Minimum Viable Product: a guide", *Startup Lessons Learned* (his blog), Monday 3 Aug 2009. https://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/08/minimum-viable-product-guide.html [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** "The Lean Startup | Methodology", https://theleanstartup.com/principles [practitioner direct: the book's official site; parts are written in the third person ("as Ries terms them")]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Not opened:** *The Lean Startup* (2011).

Quotes:

- (a) "First, a definition: the minimum viable product is that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort."
- (a) "Some caveats right off the bat. MVP, despite the name, is not about creating minimal products." … "Second, the definition's use of the words maximum and minimum means it is decidedly not formulaic. It requires judgment to figure out, for any given context, what MVP makes sense."
- (a) "In retrospect, two weeks was way too long. We could have found out that nobody wanted the product a lot sooner. At a minimum, a simple AdWords smoke test would have revealed how utterly bad the concept was."
- (b) "The fundamental activity of a startup is to turn ideas into products, measure how customers respond, and then learn whether to pivot or persevere. All successful startup processes should be geared to accelerate that feedback loop."
- (b) "The unit of progress for Lean Startups is validated learning-a rigorous method for demonstrating progress when one is embedded in the soil of extreme uncertainty."

**Summary.** The MVP is defined by what it teaches, not by how small it is: the version that yields the most validated learning about customers for the least effort. Build-measure-learn is the loop that MVP feeds. Build something, measure how customers respond, decide to pivot or persevere, and make the loop faster. Progress is counted in validated learning, not in product shipped.

**Misreading to avoid.** MVP as "version 1 with fewer features" or "the cheapest thing we can release". Ries says in the same post that it "is not about creating minimal products", that it adds overhead ("MVP is quite annoying, because it imposes extra overhead"), and that it can be a smoke test with no product at all.

**Drift.** (1) "MVP = minimal product" contradicts his own 2009 caveat. (2) The coinage of the term is often credited to Frank Robinson (SyncDev, 2001). That claim was **not** verified: syncdev.com's MVP page, opened today, defines the term but does not claim the coinage. Don't put the attribution on a slide without a primary.

## 7. Jeff Gothelf & Josh Seiden: Lean UX hypotheses

**Primary (a):** Jeff Gothelf, "The Hypothesis Prioritization Canvas", 8 Nov 2019. https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/the-hypothesis-prioritization-canvas/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** Jeff Gothelf, "How to use the Lean UX Canvas", 28 Jun 2021 (video transcript, written as the 3rd edition of *Lean UX* with Seiden went to press). https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/how-to-use-the-lean-ux-canvas/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (c):** Jeff Gothelf, "Lean UX Canvas V2", 9 Sep 2019. https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/leanuxcanvas-v2/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06. The page now carries an update note that the canvas has since become the Lean Product Canvas and Lean Strategy Canvas.
**Failed:** https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/lean-ux-hypothesis-template/ returns **404**.
**Not opened:** *Lean UX* (any edition).

Quotes:

- (a) "While there are many templates, the one I've been teaching for the past few years looks like this: We believe [this outcome] will be achieved if [these users] attain [a benefit] with [this solution/feature/idea]."
- (a) "I like this template because the act of filling it out is the first test of the hypothesis. If you and your team can't complete this template in a way that you believe that's a good indication you shouldn't be working on that idea."
- (b) "It's amazing that at this point, we're declaring assumptions as we work through the canvas. We've got all the pieces now that we need to write our hypothesis statement." … "Not coincidentally, every one of those variables, in brackets, lines up to box 2, box 3, box 4, and box 5."
- (b) On box 2: "Business outcomes are just regular old outcomes. You could call them key results. Most importantly, you should call them measurable changes in human behavior."
- (b) Box 8: "What's the least amount of work we need to do to learn the next most important thing?"
- (c) Worked example: "We believe a 25% reduction in administration costs will be achieved if Rochelle the System Admin spends less time configuring users with a batch configuration tool."

**Summary.** The hypothesis is assembled from assumptions the team has already declared: the business outcome, the users, the benefit they get, and the solution. The template ties those four together in one sentence, and a team that cannot fill it in convincingly has learned something before testing. Hypotheses are then sorted by risk and perceived value; only high-risk, high-value ones earn discovery time, and the experiment is the least work that teaches the next most important thing.

**Misreading to avoid.** Writing the hypothesis around the feature ("We believe adding X will…") with the users and benefit left implicit. The template carries four slots, and the outcome slot is a behaviour change (box 2), not a delivery milestone. Also: not every hypothesis gets tested. In the prioritization canvas, "High value, low risk hypotheses don't require discovery work… We build these ideas", and low-value, high-risk ones "are thrown away".

**Drift.** The format many slides show is "We believe [X]. We will know we're right when we see [signal]", often credited to *Lean UX* (2013). That wording was **not** found on any Gothelf page opened today (the dedicated template page 404s). Gothelf's own current template (2019 onward) has no "we'll know we're right" clause; the success measure lives in the canvas's outcome box instead. If a slide wants a pre-stated signal line, cite O'Reilly's HDD template (pack 1 §2), not Gothelf.

## 8. Jakob Nielsen: why you only need to test with 5 users

**Primary:** Jakob Nielsen, "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users", Nielsen Norman Group, 18 Mar 2000. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Underlying study (not opened):** Nielsen & Landauer, "A mathematical model of the finding of usability problems", *Proceedings of ACM INTERCHI'93*, pp. 206–213, cited on the page.

Quotes:

- "Elaborate usability tests are a waste of resources. The best results come from testing no more than 5 users and running as many small tests as you can afford."
- "The typical value of L is 31%, averaged across a large number of projects we studied." (L = the share of problems one user reveals; the formula itself is an image on the page, a diminishing-returns curve.)
- "The curve clearly shows that you need to test with at least 15 users to discover all the usability problems in the design. So why do I recommend testing with a much smaller number of users?" … "Let us say that you do have the funding to recruit 15 representative customers and have them test your design. Great. Spend this budget on 3 studies with 5 users each!"
- "You need to test additional users when a website has several highly distinct groups of users. The formula only holds for comparable users who will be using the site in fairly similar ways."

**Summary.** Each additional test user reveals fewer new problems, so five comparable users find most of what a study will find (the page says 85%). The point of the article is iteration: spend the budget on several small rounds and fix the design between them. Fifteen users in one study find nearly everything, but three five-user rounds with fixes in between improve the design more.

**Misreading to avoid.** "Five users is enough", full stop. Nielsen's own article names the limits: comparable users only; more groups means 3–4 per group with two groups, or 3 per group with three or more; and the follow-ups linked from the page set different numbers for different methods ("Quantitative Studies (usability metrics): Test 20 Users", "Card Sorting: Test 15 Users").

**Drift.** (1) The popular line keeps "5 users" and drops "running as many small tests as you can afford". In Nielsen's own argument the five only works as part of iteration. (2) "5 users find 85% of problems" rests on L = 31%, an *average* across his projects. In a product where one user reveals less, five users find less. (3) It is a claim about qualitative usability testing of one design, not about interviews, surveys or discovery research.

## 10. L. David Marquet: intent-based leadership

**Primary (a):** https://davidmarquet.com/ [practitioner direct], home page section "What is Intent-Based Leadership?". Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (b):** https://davidmarquet.com/my-story/ [practitioner direct]. Opened 2026-10-06.
**Primary (c):** "Push Authority to Information", davidmarquet.com, 18 Nov 2015 [practitioner direct]. Live URL returns **404**; read 2026-10-06 in Wayback: https://web.archive.org/web/20190716173105id_/https://www.davidmarquet.com/2015/11/18/push-authority-to-information/.
**Primary (d):** "Ladder of Leadership", davidmarquet.com [practitioner direct]. Live URL **404**; read 2026-10-06 in Wayback: https://web.archive.org/web/20201023051607id_/https://www.davidmarquet.com/ladderofleadership/.
**Not opened:** *Turn the Ship Around!* (2012).

Quotes:

- (a) "Intent-Based Leadership (IBL)® is a language for high performing teams used on nuclear submarines which enables both distributed decision-making and organizational alignment. The language stresses the word intent, where leaders convey to teams what they are trying to achieve (organizational intent), and decision-makers report how they intend to achieve these goals."
- (a) "Intent differs from the normal permission because with intent, the default is action absent a veto whereas with permission the default is stasis absent approval."
- (a) "Since "I intend to…" is level 5 on the Ladder of Leadership® we say, "Strive for Five." The result is a leader-leader organization rather than a leader-follower organization, where everyone thinks of themselves as a leader, leading toward organizational success."
- (b) "Captain Marquet began treating his crew as leaders, not followers, and giving control, not taking control."
- (c) "Most hierarchies operate like this; the people at the bottom have the information but not the authority to make decisions. So we create a system to channel information to the authority." … "The correct way is this; we take the authority for making decisions and we push it down to the people with the information."
- (d) "The first rung of the ladder is "tell me what to do"". The ladder is described as "the seven rung ladder", "Developed with Dr. Stephen Covey".

**Summary.** Leaders state what they are trying to achieve; the people closest to the work state what they intend to do about it, and act unless someone stops them. Decision authority moves down to where the information already is, rather than information moving up to where authority sits. The ladder gives the language for each step from "tell me what to do" upward.

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating "I intend to…" as a script that makes a team empowered. On Marquet's own page it is a language for distributed decisions *and* alignment: leaders still convey organisational intent, and the default flips from waiting for approval to acting unless vetoed. A leader who keeps every decision while asking people to say "I intend to" has changed the words but not where authority sits.

**Drift.** (1) "I intend to" is often presented as the top of the ladder. Marquet puts it at **level 5** of a **seven**-rung ladder ("Strive for Five"). (2) The brief's "giving control vs information" maps to two separate lines on his pages: "giving control, not taking control" (live, my-story) and "push it down to the people with the information" (2015 post, Wayback only). The aphorism "don't move information to authority, move authority to the information" was not found word-for-word. (3) The book's "competence and clarity" pillars were **not** found on any page opened today; don't cite them to the site.

## 11. Alistair Cockburn: walking skeleton

**Primary:** Alistair Cockburn, "Walking skeleton", alistair.cockburn.us. The live URLs (http://alistair.cockburn.us/Walking+skeleton, https://alistair.cockburn.us/walking-skeleton/, https://alistaircockburn.com/Walking-Skeleton) all return **404** today. Read 2026-10-06 in Wayback: https://web.archive.org/web/20160210222727id_/http://alistair.cockburn.us/Walking+skeleton [practitioner direct]. The page quotes his own *Crystal Clear* (2004).
**Not opened:** *Crystal Clear* (2004).

Quotes:

- "A Walking Skeleton is a tiny implementation of the system that performs a small end-to-end function. It need not use the final architecture, but it should link together the main architectural components. The architecture and the functionality can then evolve in parallel."
- "The walking skeleton is not complete or robust (it only walks, pardon the phrase), and it is missing the flesh of the application functionality."
- "A walking skeleton is different from a spike. A spike is "the smallest implementation that demonstrates plausible technical success."" … "A walking skeleton, on the other hand, is permanent code, built with production coding habits, regression tests, and is intended to grow with the system."
- "I first found this pattern or strategy around 1994, named it somewhere between then and 1997." Later on the same page: "I coined the term Walking Skeleton in the late-1990s sometime, without knowing about the Basili-Turner paper, but it is the same thing" (Basili & Turner, 1975, used "skeleton").

**Summary.** The walking skeleton is the first thing built: a thin thread of real, production-quality code that runs end to end through every main architectural part. It does very little, but it proves the parts connect, and from then on features and architecture grow on it together. What it looks like depends on the system: "For a client-server system, it would be a single screen-to-database-and-back capability."

**Misreading to avoid.** Treating it as a throwaway prototype or a spike. Cockburn separates the two explicitly: a spike is thrown away, a skeleton is permanent and tested. It is also not an MVP. Its purpose is to connect the architecture and test "the team, the process, the technologies, and the architecture at a very early point", not to learn whether customers want the product.

**Drift.** (1) Cross-pack: Patton (pack 1 §3) cites the term for "the smallest possible system you could build that would give you end to end functionality", a user-facing release slice. Cockburn's original is architecture-first and engineering-facing. On a product slide, say whose sense is meant. (2) Cockburn himself dates the naming loosely ("between then and 1997" / "late-1990s") and points to the 1975 Basili–Turner "skeleton". Say "late 1990s", not a year.

## 12. Liberating Structures: 1-2-4-All and TRIZ (now "Creative Destruction")

**Primary (a), live:** https://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all [practitioner direct] (`/1-1-2-4-all/` redirects here). Opened 2026-10-06. The current page merges in-person and online versions; bracketed text such as "[breakouts]" is the page's own online adaptation.

**Earlier versions (Wayback, read 2026-10-06):** https://web.archive.org/web/20210102155827id_/http://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-1-2-4-all/ and https://web.archive.org/web/20210113114553id_/http://www.liberatingstructures.com/6-making-space-with-triz/.
Both structures: "Liberating Structure developed by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless."

Quotes, 1-2-4-All (live):

- "In 1-2-4-All, participants first work alone, second in pairs, then in small groups, to generate solutions to a specific challenge. It engages every voice regardless of group size, generating a kaleidoscopic view of perspectives, and expresses ideas people would otherwise keep to themselves."
- Steps: "Intro: Share the structuring invitation and identify the shared challenge. (1 min.)" / "Individual Reflection: Individuals silently reflect and write down ideas. (1 min.)" / "Pair Up: Participants form pairs to share and build on ideas, trying to defer judgment and keep each idea distinct [breakouts of two or three]. (2 min.)" / "Form Quartet: Pairs join to form quartets to refine and differentiate ideas. [Manually merge pairs into quartets.] (5 min.)" / "All-Together Sharing: Everyone returns to plenary. A few people share something everyone should hear. […] (7 min.)"
- "Stick to precise timing and facilitate quiet reflection. As host, don't over-help or overcontrol the group's conversations."

Quotes, 1-2-4-All (2021 Wayback version, "(12 min.)"): "Silent self-reflection by individuals on a shared challenge, framed as a question … 1 min." / "Generate ideas in pairs, building on ideas from self-reflection. 2 min." / "Share and develop ideas from your pair in foursomes (notice similarities and differences). 4 min." / "Ask, "What is one idea that stood out in your conversation?" Each group shares one important idea with all (repeat cycle as needed). 5 min."

Quotes, Creative Destruction / TRIZ (live, "(~50–55 min.)"):

- "In Creative Destruction, groups imagine how to achieve the worst possible results. By asking "What must we stop doing to make progress on our deepest purpose?" participants can have fun, courageous conversations about letting go."
- Steps: "List Ideas: … Participants work alone to list everything they can do to achieve the worst result. (3 min.)" / "Combine Lists: Participants form groups of four to seven … and combine their lists. (10 min.)" / "Reckoning: … Groups identify everything they are currently doing that in any way resembles the items on their list. (10 min.)" / "Destruction: … Groups decide what they need to stop doing to achieve desired results. They identify first steps to stop each counterproductive behavior … (10 min.)" / "All-Together Sharing: Groups share their lists of what to stop … (10–15 min.)" (after a 5-minute intro).
- "Be sure participants identify behaviors to stop doing, not new things to start doing, and warn them to be wary of snapback to counterproductive behaviors."

Quote, TRIZ (2021 Wayback, "Making Space with TRIZ", 35 min.): "Attribution: * Inspired by one small element of the eponymous Russian engineering approach teoriya resheniya izobretatelskikh zadatch."

**Summary.** 1-2-4-All has everyone think alone in silence, then build in pairs, then refine in fours, then share with the whole room. Every voice gets in before the loudest ones settle the question. TRIZ (now Creative Destruction) asks a group to design the worst possible result, then admit which of those things they already do, then decide what to stop and the first step to stopping it.

**Misreading to avoid.** 1-2-4-All: skipping the silent minute, or running the "All" step as every group reporting back. The current page says "A few people share something everyone should hear" (the 2021 version: "Each group shares one important idea"). TRIZ: stopping at the funny worst-case list. The work is in the second step (what we do today that resembles the list) and the third (what we stop). The tip says the output is things to *stop*, "not new things to start doing."

**Drift.** (1) The pattern's timings changed. The 2021 page ran 1/2/4/5 minutes (12 min), so the minutes happened to match the group sizes and "1-2-4" got read as minutes. The live page runs 1+1+2+5+7 (~15 min). The numbers are group sizes. (2) TRIZ has been renamed "Creative Destruction (aka TRIZ)", lengthened from 35 to ~50–55 min, and now starts with each person listing alone before groups combine. The 2021 version used 1-2-4-All for every step. (3) Liberating Structures' TRIZ is not the Russian engineering method: its own earlier attribution says "Inspired by one small element" of it.
